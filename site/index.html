LUCID · feedback-driven domain randomization for humanoid motion tracking · Unitree G1 · SONIC / Isaac Lab · updated 2026-09-04

Is the robot getting better, or is its training getting easier?

An adaptive curriculum that may narrow its own training ranges will narrow them when learning stalls. Training reward goes up, the dashboard looks healthy, and the policy has quietly stopped seeing the physics it will face at deployment. We measured that failure in long humanoid training runs, priced it, removed it with a one-line rule, audited which signals can be trusted, and replayed the policies in a second simulator so you can watch the difference.

The problem

A curriculum that grades its own exam

Domain randomization trains a policy on ranges of mass, friction, center of mass, joint offsets, actuator delay, and pushes, so that the real robot lands inside the training distribution. The width of the ranges is a genuine tension: too wide and learning stalls, too narrow and the policy is brittle. Adaptive curricula resolve it by moving the ranges during training, usually driven by some score measured on the current training batch.

The catch is that the score is measured on a distribution the curriculum controls. If the score falls and the curriculum is allowed to make training easier, the score recovers with no change to the policy. Repeat, and the training ranges drift away from deployment while every monitor improves. We call the end state training-range collapse.

Return went up in exactly the runs that had stopped training on the physics that mattered.

Our testbed is SONIC whole-body motion tracking on a 29-DoF Unitree G1 in Isaac Lab: 1,024 environments, 8,000 PPO iterations from scratch, one training clip, six randomized channels scaled by one number λ where λ = 1 is the training envelope. Every policy is scored afterwards on a frozen physics ladder (λ from 0 to 2, 512 episodes per cell) that no curriculum ever sees.

2 · A one-line rule removes the failure

Keep the same controller, refuse every requested decrease. Across three seeds the rule blocked 453, 951, and 629 reductions and applied none. Under a comparison rule frozen before the data existed, the resulting policies are within margin of fixed domain randomization on all four outcome components and all three seeds. They are not better. Three seeds do not establish equivalence either: the one-sided 95% lower bound on the difference is −3.19 points, outside the 2-point margin. The rule passing is a decision, not a demonstration that the two are equally robust.

3 · The signal was the culprit, not the gain

At fixed difficulty, a usable readiness signal must move with competence. Time-out survival does (rank correlation +0.99 with training progress). The learned mismatch signal that drove the collapsing curriculum does not (−0.04, nineteen direction reversals), and its direction was set by the arm rather than the policy. No tuning of the controller could have saved it. Survival is usable, but only if measured on the proposed harder conditions rather than the current ones, and only if a failed test can hold but never shrink the ranges.

4 · Robustness is per-parameter, and combinations bite

Widening one channel at a time on frozen policies (512 episodes per cell) shows that pushes are the binding channel for every randomized policy, while mass, center of mass, and joint offsets can be tripled at under 1.4 points of cost. The unrandomized policy fails first under center-of-mass shift instead. Widening everything together costs about six points more than the sum of the parts. Both facts argue for expanding channels separately and testing corners.

How the curriculum works

Expand on evidence, never shrink

What the collapsing version did

A PI controller read a learned mismatch statistic on the current training batch and moved the single scale λ up or down toward a set point. When learning stalled, lowering λ restored the statistic faster than learning could, so the controller lowered it, and lowered it again.

What the never-shrink version does

Same controller, same signal, one projection: a requested decrease is refused and logged. The range may expand or hold. Across three seeds this alone recovered the robustness of fixed randomization.

What the range-expansion curriculum adds

  • Test proposed conditions before expanding. A probe set of 128 environments trains one step beyond the active range; the range steps up only when probe survival clears a threshold over a window.
  • Let each physical parameter expand on its own clock. Pushes may stall at 1× while mass and center of mass go to 3×.
  • Check combined changes. A periodic probe at the corner of all proposed ranges can hold an expansion that passes channel by channel but fails together.
  • Keep practicing earlier conditions. A quarter of environments rehearse earlier ranges; admitted ranges never shrink.

A scalar version of the probe gate, trained from scratch, expanded λ from 1.0 to 1.5 in four evidence-triggered steps at iterations 3,362, 4,103, 4,790, and 5,891 with no decrease. It also exposed a defect: a stale global return guard froze expansion 914 times after the ceiling. The final design uses probe-relative evidence.

What would count as an RL curriculum barrier?

The actuator study asks a narrower question than the broad domain-randomization work above. We multiply all 29 joint effort limits by one common scale s, leaving every other event and actuator randomization channel at nominal and latency at zero. A low s removes the near-nominal samples that make ordinary fixed randomization an implicit curriculum. The scientific question is not merely whether a nominal policy fails there; it is whether a policy trained from scratch at that exact plant also fails while a policy staged into the same endpoint succeeds.

Frozen-policy failure locates a stress point. Only matched direct failure plus staged success can identify a curriculum barrier.

The 0.40 arm sat in the preregistered gray zone at iteration 1,500 (time_out = 0.5997), crossed the direct-learning threshold by iteration 2,000 (0.7743), and reached a trailing-50 mean of 0.9525 at iteration 6,000. It therefore closes 0.40 as hard but directly learnable for this seed and clip. The same checkpoint's 511/512 completion at 0.30 is also an empirical feasibility witness: the earlier conjecture that 0.35 or 0.30 was physically impossible is false in this tested setting.

Completion is not nominal-quality imitation. At nominal torque both checkpoints complete 512/512, but global MPJPE is 262.7 mm for the 0.40-trained policy and 91.4 mm for the nominal-trained policy. We therefore carry tracking error beside survival and do not call the adapted gait equivalent.

Hardware not run—Nothing has run on the robot
Why none of this has beaten fixed randomization. Fixed randomization here is already a curriculum. Every range shrinks toward its nominal, so the support at any intensity sits inside the support at the maximum, and every environment redraws at each episode reset. A batch at full intensity therefore contains near-nominal episodes, and staging cannot withhold what fixed randomization keeps supplying. That is one structural fact rather than a run of unlucky designs, and it predicts when a curriculum could help: only when the target distribution is concentrated, so the easy cases are genuinely absent.
What we do not claim. No result here shows that any curriculum makes the policy better. No feedback method has beaten fixed randomization on our evidence. The never-shrink rule is essentially the boundary rule of Automatic Domain Randomization, and a published multi-dimensional curriculum already combines several randomization parameters, learning history, progress-driven allocation and expansion-only support, so we claim none of those; our contribution is the measurement of what goes wrong without the rule, the audit of which signals can drive it, and the per-channel structure of humanoid robustness. All capability results come from one training clip. Between-seed offsets reach 7.8 points, so single-seed screens rank designs and do not decide.

Next steps

How we plan to improve performance, in order of information per GPU-hour

The order changed again on 2026-09-04 because the practice-productivity experiment is already complete. Push is both the largest measured single-channel loss and trainable by extra exposure: dedicated Push 3× practice improved the matched cell by 9.18 points and transferred above the practised level. The remaining uncertainty is sharper: on the broad suite, choosing push did not beat allocating the same hard-example density to the manageable-channel placebo. A scheduler must therefore beat a fixed reallocation and a frozen preset, not merely show that hard practice helps.

Measured rates on the single RTX 5080: a from-scratch 8,000-iteration arm takes about 5.4 GPU-hours, a 1,500-iteration warm-start arm about one, an evaluation cell about 33 seconds. Everything below is serial on that card.
