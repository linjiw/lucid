Difficulty Evacuation

A feedback-driven domain-randomization curriculum for humanoid motion tracking learns to run away from the conditions it was built to master. We measure how often it happens, what it costs, and why the obvious fix earns a smaller claim than it first appears to.

Sim-to-real transfer for humanoids needs wide domain randomization, and nobody knows the right width. The appealing answer is to let the policy choose: measure how it is doing and move difficulty accordingly. We show that this fails in a specific, reproducible way.

In six from-scratch 8,000-iteration runs of a PI controller driving a scalar difficulty λ from a learned latent signal, two runs evacuated difficulty entirely — ending at λ = 0.062 and λ = 0.012 after having held λ = 1.0 for thousands of iterations. All six moved difficulty down at some point. The failure is not tied to one arm or one seed: seed 8600 destroyed the stratified arm while seed 8601 destroyed the unstratified one.

The retreat is expensive and it is invisible to the obvious monitor. We predicted the cost before measuring it, froze the prediction while the confirmation was still training, and then scored the collapse run: predicted 0.725 frontier success AUC, observed 0.740. Against contemporaries on its own seed that arm is 14.19 points below fixed randomization and 17.32 points below the ratcheted version of the same controller. Meanwhile training return rose: across twelve scored arms, the two highest terminal returns in the entire campaign belong to exactly the two arms that evacuated. Return is not a weak curriculum signal. It is an inverted one.

A monotone projection — refuse every downward request, keep the emergency brake — deletes the failure by construction, and three seeds now confirm it: 2,033 downward requests refused, zero applied. We are careful about what that buys. Because the constrained controller reaches λ = 1.0 and stays there, its applied difficulty is identical to fixed randomization for 98.75% of training, so the noninferiority test it passes is close to tautological. The +3.1-point gain that a single seed showed is now settled as noise: across three seeds the ratchet-minus-fixed deltas are −0.16, +3.13 and −1.17 points, and two of the three favour fixed. The contribution is a deleted failure mode, not a better policy.

01

What evacuation looks like

λ scales six randomization channels at once — link mass, friction, restitution, centre-of-mass offset, joint-default bias, and push disturbances — around their nominal values. At λ = 0 the environment is deterministic; at λ = 1 every range is at its configured maximum. The controller may move λ each iteration.

Every trace below is real per-iteration telemetry from a completed 8,000-iteration run.

02

Why a controller retreats

The controller tracks an error between a target and a measured "gap" from a frozen temporal autoencoder, and integrates it. The intended reading is that a large gap means the policy is struggling and difficulty should ease.

The problem is that the signal is not anchored to competence. That is now measured rather than argued. Across five runs whose applied λ is pinned at 1.0 — three fixed-DR arms and two ratchet arms, where difficulty is constant by construction and competence is the only thing left moving — the rank correlation of each signal against the training iteration is:

Body-grounded signals do not rescue the design. The natural objection to the table is that the gap is a learned statistic and return is a reward; a signal read off the body — foot slip under load, actuator saturation, mechanical work — ought to be harder to fool. All of those are already logged population-wide every iteration, so the question was answerable from existing telemetry. Foot slip is the best of them and it is still weaker than survival: at fixed λ = 1 it falls as the policy improves (the right sign, on every run) but with 17 direction reversals, and it saturates — six percent of its whole-run range is traversed in the last quarter of training. What it does have is authority: across fourteen frozen-policy ladders its rank correlation with evaluation difficulty is +1.00 on every one, and during both collapses it responded to the actuator with r = +0.75 and +0.71 where the latent gap read about zero. But the sign of that response is the inverting one — cutting λ relieved slip by 34% and 11% — so a bidirectional controller on foot slip would have evacuated too. Torque saturation and undesired contact flip sign from arm to arm. Mechanical work and action rate rise with competence: a better tracker does more work, so an "actuator margin" read from them would call the best policy the most exhausted. The conclusion is the one the module already encodes: measure survival where you want to go, under an actuator that cannot retreat. (Receipt: lucid_physical_signal_audit_20260902.json.)

The gap does not track competence at all, and the disqualification holds however the controller consuming it is tuned. Worse, its direction is set by the arm rather than by the policy: the same correlation is −0.66 in the no-randomization arm, about zero in the fixed arms, and +0.39 and +0.50 in the two arms that evacuated — in those two the gap rose while λ was being cut, which is exactly the sign that drives a PI controller to cut further. One correction this audit forced on us: mean return is anchored at fixed difficulty. It fails for a different reason, being unbounded and scale-drifting from 1.4 to about 12 across a run, so no fixed threshold on it has a stable meaning.

As the policy improves, its latent statistics drift and the measured gap drifts with them. In the seed-8600 collapse the gap's 90th percentile rose from 0.435 to 0.796 while the policy was getting better, crossed the target near iteration 7,000, and the controller responded by cutting λ from 0.96 to 0.15 over the final 1,000 iterations. Zero guard trips. The brake never fired because nothing was breaking — by the controller's own instrument, everything was fine.

Why the retreat is total, not partial

The telemetry says something worse than "it chased a drifting signal." Cutting difficulty does not reduce the controller's own error. Over the collapse window of lucid_s4_rg s8600 the correlation between applied λ and the measured gap is r = −0.201 — a working controller needs r > 0. In lucid_rg s8601 the gap climbs from 0.680 to 0.814 while λ is cut from 0.99 to 0.23, and in the final 1,000 iterations it is still above its 0.778 target 474 times at λ ≈ 0.2.

An inverted signal settles somewhere: as difficulty falls the signal improves until the loop balances. A signal the actuator cannot move gives the integrator no restoring force at all, so λ winds to the rail. That is why both collapses end near λ = 0.01 rather than at a partial retreat.

The three candidate signals therefore fail differently, and the distinction decides what to build next. The latent gap has no authority and the wrong sign. Training return is inverted. Episode survival is inverted and saturates near 0.95 after iteration 5–6k — the collapsed arm scores 0.988 against fixed's 0.948. A difficulty controller needs a signal whose response to its own actuator is both non-trivial and correctly signed. None of the three is, and two reward evacuation outright.

03

What it costs

We score policies on a held-out ladder of fresh physics draws: nine cells from λ = 0 to λ = 2.0, 512 episodes each. Cells above λ = 1 are outside anything the policy trained on. The headline endpoint is a normalized trapezoidal AUC over the four frontier cells.

The monitor you would reach for is inverted

The natural thing to watch is training return. It points the wrong way.

05

The fix, and the smaller claim it earns

The intervention is one line of intent: λ may rise but never fall by controller request. The relative-return guard stays as the only legal brake, so a genuine catastrophe can still lower difficulty; a drifting signal cannot.

λk+1 = max( λk, clip( λk + αuk, 0, 1 ) )

Mechanically it works, and it is not a no-op: across three runs the controller requested 453, 951 and 629 decreases — 2,033 in total, every one refused. Zero guard trips, zero applied decreases, zero unguarded decreases, zero evacuations, and a terminal high-λ fraction of 1.000 on all three seeds.

The honest limitation, stated up front

Because the constrained controller reaches λ = 1.0 by iteration ~78 and the projection pins it there, its applied λ has exactly one distinct value over iterations 101–8,000. It and fixed randomization train on an identical distribution for 98.75% of training, differing only by a brief warm-up ramp and RNG divergence.

Two things follow. A noninferiority test against fixed is close to structurally guaranteed and carries little information. And the +3.125-point frontier gain that one seed showed is now settled across three. The ratchet-minus-fixed deltas are −0.163, +3.125 and −1.172 points — mean +0.597, paired SD 2.247, and two of the three seeds favour fixed. The frozen decision passed on noninferiority and the receipt records superiority_claim_authorized: false.

The right comparator is therefore not fixed randomization. It is the unconstrained controller, which fails catastrophically 2 times in 6.

06

A hypothesis we refuse to call a result

Ordering the arms by how much difficulty they actually trained under predicts their frontier robustness well. Fitting frontier AUC on a recency-weighted exposure integral gives R² = 0.988 across a 41-point range.

We do not believe that number, and the preregistration says so.

Why R² = 0.988 is not evidence

The five non-stratified points alone — one at zero exposure, four clustered at full exposure — give residual SD 0.0157, which is exactly the replicate noise floor. Adding the two points that actually exercise the dose model raises residual SD to 0.0183. The line is pinned by a two-support design; R² measures that geometry. Lack-of-fit F(2,3) = 1.90 against Fcrit 9.55: the linear form cannot be rejected, and neither can anything else.

Recency was not identified either. A plain uniform mean scores 0.981, and a rectangular trailing window beats the exponential kernel out of sample (LOO Q² 0.977 vs 0.969). Exactly one arm in the fit is stratified, so the dose scalar is one free parameter from one run.

The discriminating measurement has since been made. Scoring the collapse run returned 0.740: inside the recency band [0.674, 0.776], below the uniform band [0.761, 0.890], and outside the [0.761, 0.776] overlap where the design would have discriminated nothing. Recency is the surviving law; "evacuation is free" is rejected. One out-of-sample point still does not earn the phrase "recency-weighted", and a second defect has since appeared that matters more: the law has no seed term. Pooled seed means of frontier AUC are 0.904, 0.898 and 0.826, so the between-seed effect is 7.8 points — roughly four times the law's residual SD. It was invisible in the original fit because that fit held only seeds 8600 and 8601, which differ by about 0.6 points.

07

An endpoint that stops being held out

The natural next experiment is to widen the training envelope past λ = 1 and see whether robustness follows. Setting that up surfaced a measurement problem we would otherwise have walked into, and it generalizes well beyond this project.

Our frontier endpoint averages four cells at λ = 1.25 to 2.0. That is genuinely held out for a policy trained at λ ≤ 1. It is not held out for a policy trained at λ = 1.5.

The fix needs no new evaluation cells: score on {phys_175, phys_200}, which sits strictly outside every arm's training support and is already in the panel we run. It costs about 16% of endpoint resolution in spread-per-noise terms, which is the honest price of an uncontaminated comparison. We had initially written that it "costs no resolution"; an adversarial pass showed the spread grows 18.5% while the noise grows 40.9%, and we filed a correction before any data was read out.

08

Proposed next experiments

Ordered by information per GPU-hour. The ordering is not a preference — the instrument is byte-pinned, so changing the evaluator before step 1 would break the very tool step 1 needs.

  1. Score the collapse run. — DONE. Observed 0.740 against a frozen recency prediction of 0.725. Evacuation is not free; the framing holds. The same pass scored the three other unscored controller finals, which completed the return-inversion figure at twelve arms and exposed the exposure law's missing seed term.
  2. Widen the training envelope. — IN FLIGHT. A five-arm screen at λmax = 1.5 (probe-gated frontier, matched open-loop ramp, fixed 1.5, a 75/25 frontier/retention mixture, and fresh fixed 1.0), one seed, scored on the uncontaminated band. This is where a method could genuinely beat fixed randomization, because at λ > 1 something has to decide when extrapolation is safe. The decisive contrast is gate against ramp: beating fixed would only show that difficulty rose.
  3. Retention curves from the mid-training capsules already on disk (~26 GPU-min per seed). These are the only way to identify the recency term, and the capsules exist.
  4. Held-out motions. — STARTED. One of three sibling clips is scored at k128, and the arm ordering survives the change of motion (Spearman 0.8 at λ = 1.0, 1.0 at λ = 1.5). Two clips remain. This is near-motion evidence: all four clips are walking motions from one partition and no arm trained on the new ones, so it bounds the memorization worry without supporting a generalization claim.
  5. A gate that cannot invert. — BUILT. The frontier only rises; a 12.5% probe stratum runs one step above it and the frontier steps up only when the probe's episode time-out rate clears a threshold over a trailing window. The signal is population-wide, bounded, and outcome-linked, and it is read where we want to go rather than where we are, so it cannot be improved by making the current level easier. A per-channel variant — one frontier per randomization channel, the single probe visiting channels in rotation, the same ceiling on every channel — is built and tested; whether it earns a run is decided by the single-channel attribution below.
  6. Which physics breaks the policy. — DONE. The scalar ladder widens five channels at once, so a drop at phys_150 could not say which one. Scoring five seed-8600 finals on eleven cells that widen one term while the other four sit at their training envelope answers it. For the three healthy DR-trained policies the surface is anisotropic and the axis is push disturbance: at twice the range push costs 6–8 points, friction 2–4, and mass, centre-of-mass and joint offsets under 1.5 each; at three times the range the last three are still ≥ 0.938 while push is 0.705–0.770. Friction alone at 1.5 costs 2.7 points, so the largest single-cell drop of the ladder is not slip. The drop is also super-additive: at 2.0 the marginals of the fixed arm sum to 0.111 of loss and the joint cell loses 0.174, so about six points is interaction that no axis-aligned probe can see. The weak policies fail elsewhere — the no-randomization arm breaks on centre of mass alone (0.654 at 2×) — which makes anisotropy a property of the policy rather than the physics, and is the case for measuring it online. What the gate is worth, and what it is not

The monotone projection deletes evacuation, but inside the λ ≤ 1 envelope it is distributionally identical to fixed randomization, so it cannot be worth anything. If feedback has a job, it is above the envelope, where nobody knows the right width. We ran twelve arms to find out. All twelve start from the same competent policy — the fixed-DR seed-8600 final, which has the λ = 1 task solved — and get 2,000 further iterations, so the only thing that differs is how support is widened.

Width is the lever; scheduling is not

The control that matters is the first row of the table body: 2,000 further iterations at the same difficulty buy 0.6 points, inside noise. The same budget at λ = 1.5 buys eight. Whatever these arms differ by, it is not how long they trained.

Against that, the probe-gated frontier at a 1.5 ceiling is worth nothing over simply being told to train at 1.5: 0.932 against 0.935. That is the honest reading of a gate whose ceiling was already safe, and it is why the interesting experiment is the one where the ceiling is not.

Where feedback earns its place

Permitted to widen to 3.0 — the level at which the single-channel attribution says push disturbance breaks a trained policy — blind width destroys the policy. It drops to 31% episode survival in training, loses 3.7 points at nominal physics, and ends up below the untrained origin on the held-out band despite having trained on that physics. It is the only arm in the campaign that is damaged.

The gated arm, given the same permission, stopped on its own and stayed healthy: +10.6 points on the wide corner and no damage. The wide cells are inside the blind arm's training support and outside the gate's, so the arm that actually trained on that physics is the one that fails there.

The probe does the stopping, and we checked by removing the alternative

The first version of this result was not clean. The gated arm stopped at 1.5, but the relative-return guard did the stopping, not the survival probe: the guard first fired 82 iterations after the last expansion and then froze expansion on 1,509 of 1,990 iterations, while the probe was still clearing its threshold on two thirds of them. The reason is structural and worth stating on its own, because any adaptive curriculum with a return-based brake inherits it — from a warm start the guard's reference is the return earned at low difficulty, which can never be recovered once difficulty rises, so the brake latches permanently. A relative guard is no better than an absolute one when its reference predates the difficulty change.

So we ran the same arm again with the guard made inert, and let the probe decide alone. It expanded seven times, on a trailing window that fell 0.936, 0.912, 0.883, 0.831, 0.813, 0.802, 0.800 — each step just clearing the 0.80 threshold — and then stopped at 1.875 when the probe at 2.0 read 0.736, withholding for the remaining 1,191 iterations. Zero guard trips, zero decreases, healthy at nominal physics, and a wide corner of 0.744 against the guarded arm's 0.732: a difference of 1.2 points inside the 2.7-point paired noise. The guard was not what protected the policy. The probe also never read above threshold at a width whose training damaged the policy, which is the admissibility condition we preregistered for it.

That is the result we set out to get: a curriculum signal that discovers, from its own training telemetry, the per-channel structure that cost 55 frozen-policy evaluation cells to measure offline. What it does not buy is a better policy. The recovered shape scores 0.735 on the wide corner against the scalar gate's 0.744, a tie; and both sit below the best width we chose by hand.

The claim this campaign supports, and the one it does not

Supported. A monotone, probe-gated frontier is insurance. Given a ceiling that is safe it matches being told the answer; given a ceiling that is destructive it stops on the plateau by itself, and the difference is 10.6 points and a damaged policy. It needs no return guard to do it. That is a property no open-loop schedule has, because an open-loop schedule reaches whatever ceiling it was given.

Not supported. That feedback finds a better width than a well-chosen fixed one. It does not: 0.896 against 0.935 on the held-out band. The performance lever in this campaign is the shape of the support, not its scheduling — the asymmetric arms, built from the offline attribution, lead both endpoints. Feedback's contribution is that it finds that shape without the 55 cells.

Every number above is one seed and one motion clip, from a warm start rather than from scratch, and two cells at 512 episodes carry about 2.7 points of paired noise. The three-arm gaps that carry the argument — 10.6, 12, and the damage at nominal physics — are several times that. The two-point differences between the gated and blind arms at 1.5 are not, and are reported as ties.

10

Why this matters beyond one curriculum

Humanoid control is contact-rich and high-frequency. Small changes in friction or latency produce large, non-smooth changes in outcome, which makes any scalar "how hard is this" estimate noisy and drift-prone. That is precisely the regime where a feedback scheduler is most attractive and least safe.

Three transferable claims:

  • Adaptive curricula need a monotonicity argument, not just a stability argument. A scheduler can be perfectly stable, never trip a guard, and still walk the training distribution somewhere useless. Stability is the wrong invariant; support retention is the right one.
  • A difficulty controller needs a signal whose response to its own actuator is both non-trivial and correctly signed. That is a stricter and more useful test than "don't use return." Return and survival are inverted — they improve when difficulty falls, so they reward evacuation. A learned reconstruction gap fails the other way: it does not respond to the actuator at all (r = −0.20), which is what turns a retreat into a rout.
  • Sim-to-real robustness claims must state the training support alongside the evaluation range. As soon as envelopes are widened — which is the whole point of domain randomization — "held-out" silently stops meaning held out. We nearly published that error ourselves.

For deployment the practical reading is narrower and more useful than a new algorithm: if you are running an adaptive randomization schedule on hardware-bound policies, log the applied support over time, and treat any sustained downward movement as an incident rather than as the system adapting.

Limitations

  • No video. Every run in this program is headless and there is no rendering path in the evaluation code. Figure 1 is real telemetry, not robot footage. Qualitative behaviour under extrapolated physics is unverified.
  • One motion carries every headline number. The 512-episode panel is 512 fresh physics draws of one training clip, so each figure above is memorized-motion-under-new-physics. A first check on one untrained sibling clip at k128 found the ordering of arms preserved (Spearman 0.8 at λ = 1.0 and 1.0 at λ = 1.5), which rules out the strongest form of the worry but is near-motion, not motion generalization: all the clips are walking motions from the same partition, and no arm was trained on them. Two of three clips remain unscored.
  • The between-seed effect is larger than any effect measured here. Frontier AUC now exists for all three seeds, and their pooled means are 0.904, 0.898 and 0.826: seed 8602 sits 7.8 points below the others for every arm. Paired within-seed noise is 2.25 points on the four-cell AUC and 2.74 on the two-cell held-out band. One seed screens; it cannot decide.
  • The exposure law has no seed term. The 7.8-point seed effect is roughly four times the law's residual SD, and it was invisible in the original fit because that fit held only seeds 8600 and 8601, which differ by about 0.6 points. Any seed offset applied now is post-hoc, estimated from two arms and applied to two others; raw residuals are reported alongside it.
  • Tracking-quality metrics are contaminated and cannot be repaired offline. No per-frame trajectories are stored, so first-termination-masked error is unrecoverable. The contamination is measurable: at λ = 2.0 failed episodes score better joint error (262) than successful ones (434), because error stops accumulating when the robot stops moving.
  • No hardware. Nothing here has been run on a G1.
  • The extrapolation ladder is not uniformly spaced. Friction's low bound clamps at λ ≈ 1.385, so past that the ladder tests grip, mass and push rather than slip, and restitution carries no information at all above λ = 2.0. The consequence is now measured: every arm's largest single-cell drop falls at phys_150, the first cell where the static-friction floor reaches 0.05, and foot-slip growth orders those drops. Treating the cells as equally spaced difficulty steps overstates the smoothness of the curve. Revised 2026-09-02: the single-channel attribution supplies the missing control, and it reverses the reading. Friction alone at 1.5 costs the fixed arm 2.7 points against the joint cell's 7.0; the channel that carries the frontier drop for trained policies is push disturbance, and about a third of the joint-cell loss at 2.0 is interaction between channels rather than any one of them. The clamp is still real and the ladder is still uneven; slip is not what it was measuring.
